# Medieval people: historically grounded character creation

Research date: 1 October 2026. Purpose: help an AI create plausible people in historical medieval settings or explicitly labeled medieval-inspired fiction. Primary coverage is Europe and the Mediterranean, with bounded comparisons to contemporary African and Asian societies. This is not a claim to exhaust a thousand years of world history.

## M01. Start with place and date

“Medieval” is a historical periodization, not a universal culture or technology level. For European orientation, roughly 500–1500 is a useful broad frame, often divided into early, high and late medieval periods. Exact boundaries depend on region and subject. The eastern Roman, Latin Christian and Islamic worlds had connected but distinct histories. [Medieval Art: historical background](https://resources.metmuseum.org/resources/metpublications/pdf/Medieval_Art_A_Resource_for_Educators.pdf)

Before creating anyone, specify:

- A year or a window of about fifty years, narrowing further for political events.
- A locality and polity as they existed then; modern country names may be orientation aids.
- Rural, urban, coastal, frontier, court, religious-house or mobile setting.
- Legal status, household position, livelihood and access to resources.
- Language practices, religious community and relevant institutions.
- Whether the scenario is historical, alternate history or fantasy inspired by history.

A person in York around 1400, Córdoba around 1000 and Cairo around 1300 should not receive interchangeable institutions, names, clothing or assumptions about law. A character in medieval Japan is not a European knight with a different sword.

Use **documented fact**, **bounded inference**, and **original invention** as separate categories. Most characters below are inventions informed by sources; their private thoughts are not historical findings. A real person's documented deed is not proof of a modern personality diagnosis.

## M02. Time periods change character possibilities

Use this as an orientation map, not a rigid European timetable:

| Broad European frame | Questions to investigate before writing |
| --- | --- |
| Early medieval, approximately 500–1000 | What survived or changed after Roman rule locally? How do courts, religious institutions, kin groups and trading networks work here? |
| High medieval, approximately 1000–1300 | What forms of urban growth, religious reform, lordship, education and regional trade affect this particular locality? |
| Late medieval, approximately 1300–1500 | What are the local effects of demographic change, plague, war, commercial networks and political contest? Which developments predate the character? |

For England, 1066 changed ruling elites and government; the circumstances around Magna Carta in 1215 differ from the labor conflicts following plague in 1348–49. Do not give all three periods one static social arrangement. [Medieval England overview](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/learn/story-of-england/medieval/introduction/), [Magna Carta records](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/explore-the-collection/explore-by-time-period/medieval/magna-carta/), [Plague and the Statute of Labourers](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/explore-the-collection/explore-by-time-period/early-modern/plague/)

Contemporary events affect people unequally. A war can be distant news for one household, an interruption to trade for another and catastrophic displacement for a third. Do not place every character at the center of the most famous event in their century.

## M03. A social position is more than a rank

Record six separate dimensions: **legal freedom, property or income, household authority, occupational expertise, institutional office and social reputation**. A prosperous dependent tenant, impoverished noble, wealthy merchant widow and respected but poorly paid scholar occupy different combinations.

Avoid using a single “feudal pyramid” as a complete description. England's governmental records, for example, distinguish institutions including the Chancery and Exchequer; lordship did not eliminate administration, records or disputes about authority. [Medieval political records](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/medieval-political-history/)

The following terms require local verification:

| Term | Working distinction | Do not assume |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Peasant | A broad description associated with rural cultivation and households. | That everyone so described is equally poor or legally unfree. |
| Free tenant | Holds land on terms distinguished from unfree status in the relevant system. | That “free” means exemption from rent, dues, law or powerful neighbors. |
| Serf / villein | Labels for forms of unfreedom and dependence; their legal meanings vary. | That one English definition applies throughout Europe or the entire period. |
| Enslaved person | A person subject to ownership and coercive control in a particular legal regime. | That this is simply another name for a tenant or a wage worker. |
| Servant | A household or employment position that needs a separate legal-status description. | That every servant is enslaved, permanently employed or socially powerless in every context. |
| Apprentice | A person training under a particular arrangement. | That modern employment terms or identical guild rules apply everywhere. |
| Noble | A locally recognized elite status. | That every noble holds a large estate, is a knight or has ready money. |
| Cleric / religious | A position within a religious institution or vocation. | That all such people possess the same training, wealth or authority. |
| Town citizen / burgess | A particular urban status where documented. | That every resident enjoys identical privileges. |

The distinction between free and unfree inhabitants is visible in Domesday records. Later English villeinage requires its own evidence rather than direct projection from 1086. [Domesday overview](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/explore-the-collection/explore-by-time-period/medieval/domesday/), [Peter Coss, “Neifs and Villeins in Later Medieval England”](https://www.reading.ac.uk/gcms/-/media/project/functions/research/graduate-centre-for-medieval-studies/documents/rms201412-p-coss-neifs-and-villeins-in-later-medieval-england.pdf)

## M04. Royals and noble households

Kingship should be located within a particular system of succession, recognition, finances, military support and religious legitimacy. A monarch's command and its enforcement are different things. The conflict producing Magna Carta in 1215 illustrates contested royal authority; it did not instantly establish modern democracy or equal rights for everyone. [Magna Carta](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/explore-the-collection/explore-by-time-period/medieval/magna-carta/)

A great household is also a workplace. The evidence used to reconstruct Countess Joan de Valence's household at Goodrich includes cooks, bakers, religious service and extensive provisioning. Such households depended on many people's labor rather than only a lord and several guards. [The People of Goodrich Castle](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/medievalhousehold)

Original characterization options, to fit to documented offices:

| Role | Actual work to define | Human tension to explore |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reigning monarch | Receiving advice, managing claims, patronage, revenue and succession. | Wants reliable information but punishes unwelcome news. |
| Queen consort | Household leadership, intercession, patronage and diplomacy where documented. | Personal ties cross political alliances. |
| Ruling queen | Authority under the relevant succession and political conditions. | Must govern through institutions while rivals dispute legitimacy. |
| Regent | Exercises specified authority during absence or minority. | Temporary responsibility becomes difficult to relinquish. |
| Royal heir | Education, representation and cultivation of supporters. | Public expectations leave little room for uncertainty. |
| Junior dynastic relative | A negotiated position in family and political networks. | Needs an independent livelihood without provoking suspicion. |
| Great landholder | Estate income, tenants, obligations and competing claims. | Hospitality and display exceed available income. |
| Minor noble | Maintaining local standing and meeting obligations. | Possesses a prestigious name but limited resources. |
| Noble widow | Management and rights that depend on local law and property arrangements. | Relatives offer “protection” that reduces autonomy. |
| Steward or estate officer | Accounting, supervision and implementation of decisions. | Must answer to both employer and local working realities. |
| Chamber attendant | Access, personal service and household routines. | Knows sensitive information but cannot safely use it. |
| Court clerk or messenger | Correspondence, recordkeeping or delivery. | An ambiguous instruction carries consequences above their rank. |

These are narrative design prompts, not claims that every court contained each exact office. Verify titles and forms of address locally. Do not automatically import an English hierarchy of dukes, earls and barons into Byzantium, a city republic or an Islamic sultanate.

Distinguish a marriage negotiation from personal feeling. Affection, ambition, religious concerns, property, family expectations and resistance can coexist. A political marriage need not mean either perfect romance or complete emotional emptiness.

## M05. Peasants, land and seasonal responsibility

For a rural character, begin with a household economy rather than dirt on their face. Ask what land or grazing rights they use, which animals they keep, what rents or services are due, how food is stored, and who provides labor. Do not assume every locality used the same open-field arrangement, crops or inheritance practice.

English evidence for unfree tenure includes specific obligations and distinctions whose meaning changed over time. Treat a claim such as “must pay a marriage fine” as something to verify for the person's status and manor, not a universal medieval rule. [Coss on English villeinage](https://www.reading.ac.uk/gcms/-/media/project/functions/research/graduate-centre-for-medieval-studies/documents/rms201412-p-coss-neifs-and-villeins-in-later-medieval-england.pdf)

Original rural roles to vary:

- A prosperous tenant lending animals to neighbors and worrying about repayment.
- A smallholder combining cultivation with seasonal wage work.
- A landless laborer relying on hiring, kin and uncertain accommodation.
- A widow negotiating who can supply labor during a peak season.
- A shepherd whose expertise is respected but whose movements attract suspicion.
- A mill worker mediating arguments about weights and queues.
- A gardener producing for a nearby town rather than only subsistence.
- A fisher whose livelihood depends on access rights and river conditions.
- A beekeeper protecting a valuable skill from a careless apprentice.
- A charcoal burner balancing fuel demand against access to woodland.
- A village officer caught between neighbors and a lord's demands.
- A migrant worker deciding whether better wages justify separation from family.

Build the seasonal calendar from the local ecology. Sowing, haymaking, harvest, animal care, maintenance and marketing create different peaks. A feast day does not eliminate urgent care for livestock or people. Nor should every scene involve hunger: successful harvests, celebrations, jokes, courtship and professional pride belong in rural life.

After the Black Death, English authorities attempted to constrain labor conditions through the 1349 ordinance and 1351 statute. This provides a specific setting for disputes over wages and mobility; it is not evidence that every European peasant immediately became free or prosperous. [National Archives plague records](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/explore-the-collection/explore-by-time-period/early-modern/plague/)

## M06. Enslavement, captivity and other coercive statuses

Slavery continued in different medieval societies. Its forms, legal definitions, routes into bondage, work and possibilities of manumission differed. The Mediterranean contained interconnected but non-identical slave systems; avoid treating one region's practice as a template for all. [Cambridge World History of Slavery, regional overview](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-world-history-of-slavery/greater-mediterranean-slave-trade/92FD7884ADEF3045D809175D925D1E25)

Three bounded reference contexts:

1. **Viking-age networks:** the National Museum of Denmark describes capture and trafficking in enslaved people, including people taken from the British Isles and eastern Europe. “Thrall” identifies an unfree status, not a fantasy species or a personality type. [Slaves and thralls](https://en.natmus.dk/historical-knowledge/denmark/prehistoric-period-until-1050-ad/the-viking-age/power-and-aristocracy/slaves-and-thralls/)
2. **Medieval England:** Domesday documents free and unfree inhabitants. Do not assume the labor categories of late eleventh-century England continued unchanged to the fifteenth century. [Domesday](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/explore-the-collection/explore-by-time-period/medieval/domesday/)
3. **Mamluk military institutions:** the Egyptian and Syrian sultanate of 1250–1517 developed from military slavery; training, manumission and advancement formed a specific elite pathway. The rise of some formerly enslaved soldiers to power does not describe the experience of all enslaved people. [The Mamluk period](https://www.metmuseum.org/fr/essays/the-art-of-the-mamluk-period-1250-1517)

For an original enslaved or captive character, define the particular coercive system before the story:

- What is known about life before bondage, and what remains unknown?
- Who claims authority over them, under what rules, and with what practical enforcement?
- What work is imposed? Which skills existed before it?
- Who can they communicate with, and what relationships do they try to preserve?
- What realistic opportunities exist for negotiation, assistance, escape, ransom or manumission?
- What would freedom require afterward: shelter, documents, income, transport, safety or reunion?

Give the person tastes, expertise, loyalties, humor and disagreements beyond victimization. Resistance may be open, covert, collective, individual or temporarily impossible. Compliance under coercion is not proof of consent or loyalty. A comparatively favorable position does not make ownership benign.

Distinguish a prisoner held for ransom, an unfree tenant, an indentured arrangement and an enslaved person. Do not call every uncomfortable obligation slavery, and do not sanitize slavery as ordinary employment. Do not assume enslavement corresponds to one modern racial category across all these places and centuries.

## M07. Towns, trades and institutions

Medieval craft associations developed unevenly; guilds also served religious and charitable functions. Not every craftsperson everywhere belonged to a uniform guild ladder. [The Met's medieval educator reference](https://resources.metmuseum.org/resources/metpublications/pdf/Medieval_Art_A_Resource_for_Educators.pdf)

Original occupational prompts:

| Occupation or position | Useful expertise | A plausible personal problem |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Weaver | Fiber quality, tension, customers, deadlines. | A cheap order damages a reputation for good work. |
| Dyer | Materials, color consistency and water requirements. | Upstream users alter water quality. |
| Tanner | Hides, processing, supply relationships. | Neighbors resent the workshop's effects. |
| Shoemaker | Fit, leather, repair and repeat clients. | A respected customer repeatedly avoids payment. |
| Mason | Materials, structure and work crews. | A patron demands unsafe speed. |
| Carpenter | Timber, joints, tools and measurements. | Good timber becomes unavailable. |
| Smith | A specific range of metalwork. | Customers wrongly assume every smith can make every weapon. |
| Baker | Fuel, grain quality, timing and customers. | A shortage creates pressure to alter quality or portion. |
| Brewer | Ingredients, vessels and a customer network. | A household business must accommodate caregiving. |
| Merchant | Credit, partners, transport and demand. | A trustworthy relative is an unreliable business partner. |
| Porter or carter | Routes, loads and local arrangements. | Competing employers demand the same day. |
| Boat worker | Current, cargo, landings and weather. | A passenger will not accept a delay. |
| Money changer | Particular currencies and exchange practices. | A disputed payment threatens reputation. |
| Notary or scribe | Documents and local procedures. | A client's instructions would harm someone unable to read them. |
| Book worker | Copying, materials, binding or decoration. | Credit for collaborative work goes to one prominent person. |
| Market seller | Seasonal supply and regular customers. | A new regulation benefits established competitors. |
| Innkeeper | Provisioning, accommodation and reputation. | A powerful guest endangers other guests' trust. |
| Midwife or caregiver | Locally learned care and household knowledge. | Experience is undervalued by a socially prestigious outsider. |
| Entertainer | Performance, audiences and patronage. | Audiences reward a joke that hurts a friend. |
| Religious-house worker | A specific practical or administrative function. | Personal vocation and daily management pull in different directions. |

These conflicts are original possibilities. Verify licenses, gender restrictions, qualifications, payment and technology in the selected locality rather than treating the table as a universal labor code.

Mediterranean commerce crossed religious and political boundaries. Venice's relationships with Islamic trading partners are one documented example; a frontier between faiths was not automatically a permanently closed border. [Venice and the Islamic World](https://www.metmuseum.org/fr/essays/venice-and-the-islamic-world-828-1797)

## M08. Women, households, age and family

Medieval women appear in English records as property managers, business operators, religious community members, workers and migrants. Widowhood could change a woman's legal and economic room for action; marital status, locality and resources mattered. This is evidence against both “women had no agency” and “all women could act independently under modern rules.” [Medieval women in archival records](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/education/teaching-resources/medieval-women/)

The British Library's medieval women exhibition documents activity in agriculture, textiles, finance, writing, religious life and politics. Margery Kempe's account and Joan of Arc's correspondence also show why “medieval woman” is not one personality. [Medieval Women: In Their Own Words](https://www.bl.uk/about/press/releases/last-chance-to-see-major-exhibition-that-tells-the-story-of-medieval-women-in-their-own-words)

For an original household, map:

- Who resides together, including servants, apprentices, lodgers and dependents.
- Who controls property, who handles cash and who actually does administrative work.
- Who cooks, cleans, obtains fuel, cares for children and supports ill or elderly members.
- How care obligations affect paid work and travel.
- Which relatives live elsewhere and how communication is maintained.
- What happens after death, remarriage, migration, apprenticeship or loss of income.

Do not assume the household is a modern nuclear family, or that every extended family lives under one roof. Do not infer a particular marriage age from a legal minimum; elite diplomatic cases are not automatically population averages. If an exact age or inheritance rule drives the plot, research the locality and social group.

Children should have age-appropriate dependence, play, learning and work rather than adult expertise in small bodies. Older people can remain active workers, managers, storytellers, claimants or caregivers. Do not turn a low historical life-expectancy figure into a claim that nobody reached later adulthood.

Give domestic relationships mixed feelings. A person can love a household and resent an unfair workload. A family business can provide security and conflict. A widow can grieve and also enjoy greater independence. A marriage can combine negotiation, affection, disappointment and practical solidarity.

## M09. Religion, learning and cultural contact

Identify the specific religious community, local institutions, rituals, languages and political conditions. Christianity, Islam and Judaism are not three internally uniform personality types. Distinguish theology, institutional policy, customary practice and an individual's understanding.

Books of hours provide evidence of structured devotion among lay Christians in late medieval Europe. They do not prove that every household owned a decorated book or followed one identical schedule. [Getty: The Medieval Bestseller](https://www.getty.edu/art/exhibitions/books/)

The Cairo Genizah preserves personal letters, legal documents and other writings in languages including Hebrew, Judaeo-Arabic, Aramaic and Arabic. This offers evidence for everyday Mediterranean relationships, not only official religious debate. [Cambridge Genizah Research Unit](https://www.lib.cam.ac.uk/collections/departments/taylor-schechter-genizah-research-unit)

Reading, writing, oral performance, numeracy and technical competence are distinct skills. A person may recognize familiar documents without composing literary prose, use a scribe, speak several languages while reading only one, or possess extensive practical knowledge without formal schooling. Define their actual competencies instead of using “illiterate” as shorthand for unintelligent.

Original religious and intellectual characters might include:

- An abbess balancing spiritual priorities and the community's finances.
- A parish priest who knows local households better than elite theological controversy.
- A lay donor who enjoys public recognition as well as sincere charity.
- A Jewish business correspondent managing trust across distance.
- A Muslim student seeking a particular teacher rather than a generic “mystic school.”
- A translator careful about what cannot be carried neatly between languages.
- A craftsperson whose religious devotion informs their workmanship.
- A skeptical believer questioning an official's conduct while retaining faith.
- A convert navigating changed community relationships without becoming a cultural blank slate.

Persecution, restrictions and violence must be grounded in a place and moment. So must cooperation. Research on thirteenth-century York identifies Jewish involvement in civic institutions, complicating a portrayal of Jewish residents only as passive victims. That evidence does not erase persecution at other times. [University of York study](https://pure.york.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/how-to-make-a-city-the-jews-the-minster-and-the-guildhall-in-thir/)

## M10. Bodies, health, clothing and physical surroundings

Historical reconstruction should account for clean water access, fuel, washing, laundry, waste disposal, housing density and resources. Avoid the assertion that medieval people never washed: documentary and visual evidence includes bathing, with practices varying by circumstance. [Getty on medieval bathing](https://www.getty.edu/news/did-medieval-people-take-baths/)

English religious houses, hospitals and almshouses provided different forms of care and shelter. These institutions were not simply modern hospitals with worse equipment, and access was not universal. Disabled people could also live within ordinary communities. [Historic England: medieval hospitals and almshouses](https://historicengland.org.uk/research/inclusive-heritage/disability-history/1050-1485/hospitals-and-almshouses/)

For characterization, record an impairment or illness only if relevant, then specify its practical effects and available support. Do not make bodily difference a visual sign of evil, a guaranteed source of mystical insight, or the person's only attribute. A character may need a stool, assistance carrying goods, a quieter workplace or a dependable walking companion while remaining skilled and socially active.

Food and clothing must be selected by period, locality, season and means. A reconstruction of an English noble household's elaborate provisioning is not a menu for all peasants. [Life in a castle](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/castles/life-in-a-castle/)

Original material-detail prompts:

- Which garment is repaired repeatedly because replacing it would be expensive?
- What is reserved for visits, worship, court appearances or festivals?
- Which tool belongs personally to the worker, and which to an employer?
- Where does the person sleep, and how much privacy is actually available?
- What smells, noises and textures come from their work rather than a generic “medieval atmosphere”?
- Does the household own a useful object jointly, borrow it or pay for its use?
- Which small luxury is worth saving for?
- What does a seasonal shortage change first: diet, heating, travel, charity or payments?

Historical healing beliefs can be described without presenting obsolete treatments as current medical advice. A character can reason carefully from incomplete knowledge. Neither modern clinical expertise nor indiscriminate incompetence should be automatic.

## M11. War, law and travel

Military life includes provisioning, transport, recruitment, maintenance, pay and relationships with civilians. Not every noble was a knight, not every armored person a noble, and properly fitted armor did not normally make its wearer helpless. [The Met: arms and armor misconceptions](https://www.metmuseum.org/toah/hd/aams/hd_aams.htm)

For a military character, establish a specific duty: mounted combat, archery, infantry service, engineering, transport, cooking, animal care, clerical work, medical assistance or household support. Do not give every soldier mastery of all weapons. A veteran's personality can reflect pride, boredom, loyalty, grief, ambition, disillusionment or several of these without reducing everyone to the same trauma narrative.

For a legal conflict, ask which authority actually handles it: a manor, town, royal officer, ecclesiastical institution or another documented jurisdiction. Identify who can testify, who preserves records, what resources each party can obtain and whether formal rights are enforceable. Do not invent a universally available police force, standardized identification system or modern prison sentence.

Travel requires a route, means, provisions, accommodation and purpose. Merchants, messengers, pilgrims, students, workers, soldiers and displaced people all offer reasons for movement. The Genizah includes accounts connected with displacement and appeals for community help, showing lives affected by war across distance. [Women and war in Genizah records](https://genizahfragments.lib.cam.ac.uk/2022/03/08/international-womens-day-2022-stories-of-women-and-war/)

Avoid universal travel speeds. A mounted messenger with support, a laden cart, a river vessel and a family travelling on foot face different constraints. Weather, terrain, water levels, security, tolls and waiting can matter more than a straight-line distance.

In narrative, distinguish a character's belief that an order is lawful from the historian's conclusion. A document can be forged; a witness can be mistaken; an official can exceed authority. These possibilities require evidence within the story rather than automatic cynicism toward all institutions.

## M13. Personalities without modern stereotypes

These original patterns can occur in many social positions. Do not assign them by nationality, religion, gender or legal status.

| Pattern | Behavioral expression | Conflict that tests it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Careful steward | Counts supplies twice and remembers who is owed. | A superior demands impressive hospitality without funding it. |
| Proud learner | Welcomes difficult work but hides elementary questions. | A mistake can harm a customer. |
| Affectionate disciplinarian | Gives practical help and strict advice. | An adult child chooses a different livelihood. |
| Skilled negotiator | Remembers concessions and people's preferred forms of respect. | A dispute has no mutually acceptable outcome. |
| Local patriot | Defends neighbors against outsiders. | A newcomer is right about a dangerous custom. |
| Devout questioner | Takes ritual seriously and challenges hypocrisy. | A respected official behaves unjustly. |
| Ambitious dependent | Seeks favor without secure resources. | Advancement requires betraying a colleague. |
| Contented specialist | Enjoys reliable work and modest pleasures. | Others demand a prestigious position they do not want. |
| Restless traveler | Collects news and enjoys unfamiliar places. | Someone at home needs predictable support. |
| Patient caregiver | Notices small changes in another's needs. | Exhaustion makes patience difficult. |
| Frugal giver | Saves carefully to support chosen people. | Several obligations become due together. |
| Socially anxious expert | Speaks confidently about work but struggles in ceremonies. | Must present a claim before powerful listeners. |
| Optimistic reformer | Believes a broken arrangement can be improved. | Well-intended change burdens an overlooked group. |
| Bitter loyalist | Complains about an institution but keeps it functioning. | Loyalty becomes complicity. |
| Status performer | Uses manners and possessions to project security. | A financial emergency threatens the performance. |
| Practical dreamer | Has an imaginative ambition and a detailed savings plan. | A family crisis consumes the savings. |
| Quick-tempered peacemaker | Dislikes injustice and intervenes rapidly. | Anger worsens a fragile negotiation. |
| Reserved humorist | Uses precise jokes with trusted company. | Someone powerful mistakes restraint for agreement. |
| Conscientious opportunist | Takes openings but honors explicit promises. | A profitable loophole harms someone vulnerable. |
| Habitual mediator | Understands several sides of a conflict. | Avoiding a decision becomes a decision itself. |
| Proud survivor | Preserves competence after loss. | Refuses help that would make recovery easier. |
| Generous patron | Funds work and enjoys admiration. | The recipient wants artistic independence. |
| Cautious dissenter | Questions authority privately and gathers support. | Waiting longer will cause preventable harm. |
| Loyal competitor | Wants to outperform a friend without losing the friendship. | Only one can receive a scarce opportunity. |

To create a distinctive person, pair one pattern with a livelihood, an obligation and a specific material problem. “A proud apprentice who conceals poor eyesight while learning delicate work” is more useful than “a mysterious medieval man.” Preserve a person's room for kindness, play and ordinary moods.

## M14. Original dossiers across social positions

The following are fictional composites. Names are writing choices, not extracted historical identities. Dates are broad placements; verify local names, offices and legal details before using these in strict historical fiction.

### 1. Lady Avice, minor landholder's widow — England, around 1300

Avice has practical estate experience and less ready money than her clothes suggest. She wants to preserve income for her household while resolving a disputed boundary. A relative offers assistance that would give him effective control. She is affectionate with old servants and too quick to dismiss younger advisers. **Competence:** knows tenants and accounts. **Limit:** needs legal assistance for a complex claim. **Hook:** a favorable witness requests an unfair concession. **Ordinary pleasure:** discussing orchard grafts with a gardener who is unimpressed by rank.

### 2. Thomas, prosperous tenant — England, around 1320

Thomas is proud of careful husbandry and relies on arrangements with neighboring households. He wants his daughter to have choices that he lacked, yet expects her to continue useful work at home. His generosity is real but often comes with advice nobody requested. **Pressure:** an animal loss makes several reciprocal obligations difficult to meet. **Hook:** share scarce help now or protect his own seasonal work. **Research boundary:** establish the manor's tenure and inheritance customs before specifying his legal rights.

### 3. Alice, landless worker — England, around 1370

Alice is observant, humorous and good at estimating what a job will actually require. She moves between opportunities but maintains close ties with a cousin's household. She wants steadier income without accepting abusive conditions. **Conflict:** a better offer requires leaving someone who provided help during a bad season. **Limit:** bargaining room does not erase legal pressure on labor after plague. **Voice:** asks about meals, lodging and payment before accepting grand promises. Her life includes courtship and friendship, not only hardship.

### 4. Matilda, brewer and household manager — an English town, around 1400

Matilda enjoys regular customers and dislikes unpredictable debt. She is patient with beginners but sharp with established people who expect exceptions. She supports an older relative and wants the household business to survive a supply problem. **Hook:** a prominent customer offers patronage in exchange for preferential treatment. **Contradiction:** values fairness but favors someone who once helped her. **Boundary:** research local brewing regulations and her legal position rather than assuming a universal right to trade.

### 5. Robert, noble household clerk — England, around 1290

Robert can prepare the documents his office requires and is vain about an elegant hand. He supports kin outside the household. **Want:** advancement through reliable service. **Conflict:** an instruction is ambiguous, and asking for clarification will annoy a powerful superior. He envies a less skilled colleague with better family connections. **Hook:** decide whether to preserve a written objection. **Limit:** literacy does not grant access to every confidential matter. **Pleasure:** collecting jokes from other workers during meals.

### 6. Agnes, convent administrator — northern France, around 1250

Agnes has a genuine religious vocation and dislikes romantic descriptions of a life she knows includes management and fatigue. She wants repairs completed before winter. A donor insists on an expensive memorial while the roof still leaks. **Competence:** patient negotiation and institutional memory. **Flaw:** delegates tasks but not authority. **Hook:** persuade the donor without making an impossible promise. **Boundary:** identify the actual order and house; do not give every convent the same rule, resources or enclosure practices.

### 7. Martin, mason with a damaged leg — a French building project, around 1380

Martin knows materials and supervises some tasks from a position that limits unnecessary climbing. He values his crew and is embarrassed by unsolicited pity. He wants an apprentice's work recognized. **Conflict:** a patron mistakes adapted working methods for incompetence. **Limit:** expertise does not eliminate pain, access problems or the need for help. **Hook:** defend a safe timetable when payment depends on visible progress. **Pleasure:** friendly arguments over whose village produces the best bread.

### 8. Elena, merchant-household manager — Venice, around 1400

Elena is calm in formal negotiations and impatient with vague news from absent partners. She wants an outstanding obligation settled without ending a valuable relationship. **Contradiction:** calculates risk well in business but repeatedly excuses a relative's unreliability. **Hook:** a letter can be interpreted as either delay or betrayal. **Limit:** verify her property and contractual authority locally. **Voice:** distinguishes what a correspondent promised from what the household merely expected.

### 9. Yusuf, textile trader — Fustat, around 1150

Yusuf values reliable partners and takes pride in recognizing quality. He is warm with guests but easily offended by public correction. He wants to expand an existing route without overextending household resources. **Hook:** decide whether a partner's silence reflects dishonesty, illness or transport disruption. **Relationships:** a knowledgeable sibling and a cautious creditor disagree. **Boundary:** his invented faith, languages and documents must be specified; the Genizah's evidence for commerce does not make every trader's life identical.

### 10. Miriam, displaced correspondent — eastern Mediterranean, late eleventh century

Miriam is trying to reconnect separated relatives and obtain dependable lodging. She is practical, affectionate and impatient with people who turn her story into a lesson about destiny. **Competence:** remembers kin connections and which promises can be relied upon. **Hook:** an offer of help requires moving farther from a hoped-for reunion. **Limit:** the letter may be dictated rather than personally written. This invented dossier draws on the existence of displacement appeals, not the biography of a specific Genizah writer.

### 11. An enslaved household worker — Viking-age Denmark, around 1000

Choose a researched personal name and place of origin rather than assigning an exotic nickname. The person retains skills, relationships and memories predating capture. They want reliable news of a relative and must assess whom it is safe to trust. **Competence:** an established craft or language, not supernatural intuition. **Hook:** another worker offers a risky opportunity to communicate. **Character range:** humor with a trusted companion and guarded behavior toward an enslaver can coexist. **Boundary:** any path to freedom must fit the local circumstances; a benevolent-owner plot is not the default.

### 12. A former military slave — Cairo, around 1350

Develop a specific recruitment, training, manumission and service history within Mamluk institutions. The adult character values comradeship, is ambitious for security and dislikes uncertainty around patronage. **Hook:** a powerful patron's interests conflict with a friend in another network. **Limit:** eventual elite service does not make the original coercion voluntary. **Ordinary detail:** takes pride in a skill unrelated to combat. Avoid projecting this exceptional pathway onto all enslaved people in Cairo.

### 13. A bookseller's assistant — Timbuktu, late fifteenth century

The assistant enjoys conversations with visitors and wants credit for careful work. A relative would prefer a different livelihood. **Hook:** a damaged item prompts a dispute about responsibility. **Competence:** knowledge of the particular shop's practices, not every scholarly tradition. **Limit:** specify the local educational network, language and material practice before adding detailed bookmaking claims. **Personality:** cheerful, proud and capable of jealousy. This is an original individual placed within a documented scholarly and commercial setting.

### 14. A workshop apprentice — Southern Song China, around 1200

Choose a locality, craft and researched name. The apprentice is ambitious, considerate toward younger coworkers and afraid of disappointing a sponsor. They want a responsibility that would demonstrate competence. **Hook:** an instruction conflicts with what an experienced worker says the material can tolerate. **Boundary:** research the actual workshop organization rather than translating a European guild's ranks. A family connection, neighborhood friendship and an ordinary leisure preference should matter alongside formal authority.

## M15. Dialogue, emotions and social conduct

Write comprehensible dialogue unless the user asks for historical language. Avoid random “thee,” “thou” and invented archaisms, especially in translated conversations that were not English. Use period-appropriate concerns and forms of respect rather than decorative old-fashioned wording.

Establish what makes speech costly. A servant's disagreement with an employer differs from an argument between siblings. Politeness can express affection, calculation, duty or self-protection. A deferential public manner need not describe a submissive private personality.

Original ways to voice the same complaint:

- An estate worker: “The account says it was delivered. The store is empty. Someone must explain the difference.”
- A dependent petitioner: “I ask only that the earlier promise be remembered.”
- A colleague: “You gave him my turn again. Tell me why.”
- A patron: “I have paid for good work. I would rather hear a delay now than an excuse afterward.”

Avoid giving every person modern political vocabulary or identical individualist goals. Also avoid making historical people incapable of affection, doubt, humor, ambition or resistance. Ask what concepts, stories and institutions would give these feelings expression in their context.

## M16. Evidence and anachronism checks

**Source limitations:** court records overrepresent disputes; elite manuscripts overrepresent people who could commission them; laws describe desired rules rather than proving universal enforcement; a satirical story may exaggerate; archaeological interpretation can remain uncertain. Combine kinds of evidence where a claim matters.

Common checks:

- Do not confuse a document's date with the event it describes.
- Do not move a late medieval or early modern object into the year 900 merely because it looks old.
- Verify crops, imported goods, weapons, printing, clocks and medical terminology for the place and date.
- Do not assume a modern nation-state, border regime or passport system.
- Do not confuse a religious ideal with everybody's behavior.
- Do not describe all trade as barter or all wealth as bags of gold.
- Do not make every village self-contained and every stranger impossible.
- Do not make every woman powerless or give every woman the same legal independence.
- Do not make every enslaved person socially identical or explain slavery through one modern racial system.
- Do not assume all healers were accused of witchcraft. The large early modern trial phenomena belong to their own dates and contexts. [National Archives witchcraft collection](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/explore-the-collection/explore-by-time-period/early-modern/witchcraft/)
- Do not turn a population average into a prediction about a particular person's life.
- Do not invent exact wages, rents, journey times or dietary quantities without a relevant source.

For a medieval-inspired fantasy, departures are allowed: gender-equal succession, universal literacy, supernatural medicine or an invented political arrangement. Label the departure and follow its consequences consistently. It should not be presented as historical evidence.

## M17. Generation procedure and reusable output

1. Establish place, date, historical mode and source coverage.
2. Define legal status independently of wealth and personality.
3. Place the person in a household and livelihood.
4. Map obligations, resources, expertise and limits.
5. Select an immediate want and a longer aspiration.
6. Add relationships with unequal and equal standing.
7. Choose one understandable contradiction and a refusal boundary.
8. Add daily routines, a pleasure and a practical material detail.
9. Check local names, titles, language, religion and technology.
10. Identify what is documented, inferred and invented.

## M18. Final review

A useful character should be recognizable without a title or costume. Their decisions should follow from circumstances and values, while their surroundings should belong to a particular historical context. Test a peaceful day, a family disagreement, a financial setback and an encounter with authority. If the character becomes a completely different person in each situation, explain the difference or revise the portrayal.

This guide provides broad coverage and selected regional modules. It does not replace focused research for a detailed reconstruction of a legal case, ritual, technical craft, particular dynasty or under-documented community. Preserve uncertainty where evidence ends; an honest gap is more useful to an AI than a confident invented fact.
